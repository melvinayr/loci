Planning a Home Solar Installation

Sizing the system

A typical household system is 6.6 kW, made up of 15 panels rated at 440 watts each. In a sunny region this produces about 26 kWh per day on average, enough to cover most of the electricity use of a family of four. To size your own system, take your annual consumption from a bill and divide it by the yearly yield of one kilowatt of panels in your area.

Roof position

In the northern hemisphere the best roof faces south, and a tilt of 30 to 35 degrees collects the most energy across the year. East and west roofs produce roughly 15 percent less but shift generation toward morning or evening, which can suit households that are away during the middle of the day. Shade from chimneys or trees matters more than most owners expect: one shaded panel in a string can drag down the whole row.

Inverters

A string inverter is the cheapest option and converts power for the whole array in one box. Microinverters sit under each panel and keep shaded panels from limiting the others, at a higher price. An inverter typically lasts about 12 years, so budget for one replacement during the 25-year warranty period of the panels.

Costs and payback

An installed 6.6 kW system costs between 9,000 and 12,000 dollars after incentives. Most households recover that cost through lower bills in 7 to 9 years. An optional 13.5 kWh battery adds around 9,500 dollars and mainly pays off where evening electricity is much more expensive than the rate paid for exported power.

Permits and installation

Local permits and utility approval take three to six weeks. The installation itself usually takes one to two days. Afterwards, the utility must inspect the system before it may be switched on.

Maintenance

Panels need little care. Rinse them with a garden hose twice a year to remove dust and pollen, and check the monitoring app monthly: a sudden drop in output on a clear day usually points to a tripped breaker or a failed inverter.
