# Cape Marrow Lighthouse

## Construction

The Cape Marrow lighthouse was built in 1874 on the northern headland of the bay, after three ships were lost on the Marrow Reef in a single winter. The engineer Halvard Pennick designed a granite tower 38 metres tall, with walls 2.4 metres thick at the base. Construction took 26 months and employed about 140 masons, most of them from the quarry town of Gilsby. The total cost was 61,000 pounds, roughly double the original estimate.

The neighbouring Pellam Point lighthouse, built in 1861 some twelve miles to the south, is often confused with Cape Marrow. It is a shorter cast-iron tower of 21 metres and was never staffed by more than two keepers.

## The light

Originally the lantern burned whale oil behind a first-order Fresnel lens that threw a white flash every 12 seconds. Paraffin replaced whale oil in 1897. The station was connected to the mains and converted to electricity in 1932, which ended the nightly lamp-trimming duties and reduced the crew from four keepers to three. The lens was kept, but a 1,000-watt bulb now sits at its centre; the beam is visible for 24 nautical miles in clear weather.

## Keepers and the Linnet wreck

Cape Marrow was staffed by keepers and their families until it was automated in 1961. The last principal keeper, Odile Strand, retired the same year after 19 years at the station. During her tenure she kept a daily weather log that is now held by the county archive.

The most famous event in the lighthouse's history was the wreck of the barque Linnet on 14 November 1889. The ship struck the Marrow Reef in a gale despite the light being clearly visible. Keeper Tobias Wren rowed out through the surf in the station's lifeboat and, over three trips, brought off 11 survivors; 7 crew members were lost. Wren received a silver medal from the Royal Humane Society.

## Today

The lighthouse was decommissioned as a navigation aid in 1998 when a modern beacon was placed on the reef itself. A museum opened in the keepers' cottages in 2004. It now receives about 41,000 visitors a year, and the climb of 189 steps to the gallery is the main attraction. A restoration of the stonework in 2019 cost 2.3 million pounds and was paid for mostly by a heritage lottery grant.
